
# Niki Interview: Key Findings


## Participant snapshot

Niki is a strong primary persona for Tee: a paying ChatGPT Pro and Claude user who already relies on AI as her main emotional support and said she would pay for something better.

- Self described introvert who found therapy access too complicated: scheduling, insurance, cost, and facing a person for 1 to 2 hours
- Has seen a therapist only 3 times, each time when sleep broke down (awake until 4 or 5am); the therapist called it a mindset issue
- Early ChatGPT Pro subscriber (2 to 3 years); dropped Gemini about 2 months ago
- Came to AI before ever trying human therapy
- Rarely tells people she uses AI this way because she worries it seems weird

## Key findings

Niki treats AI as a daily companion for emotional processing, and her biggest frustrations are about continuity, not intelligence.

### How she uses AI

- **Relationship processing** is her main use: long voice passages about interpersonal situations, asking for analysis
- **Career stress**: during her job search, ChatGPT used her CV to remind her of her wins and gave her a plan (go outside, see friends, emergency contacts)
- **Weekly check in** she set up herself: "what should I do to relieve my stress this week?"
- **Daily decisions**: shares her location, mood, and next dance class and asks what to do in between
- **Venting on the go** by voice while walking to or riding the subway; she moved almost entirely from text to voice
- Calls ChatGPT a "childhood plushie that knows me"; she has fed it her CV, portfolio, Instagram, and emotional history

### How she sees each tool

| Tool | Her role for it | Personality she perceives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ChatGPT | Primary emotional companion | Warmest; comforts first, then advises |
| Claude | Coding, design, websites | Logical and efficient, "like my father talking to me" |
| Gemini | Dropped | Middle ground, felt like an aggregator, slower |

She believes each tool's personality comes from how much personal data she has given it. Most of her friends see no meaningful difference between tools.

### Response quality

- About 60 to 70% of suggestions felt useful; the rest felt generic ("everybody knows that")
- She had to ask "what should I do next?" at every step; it never offered a full plan on its own
- When a situation recurred, ChatGPT led with more comfort, which she valued

### Pain points

- **Memory resets** across new chats; she prompts it to "search all previous tabs" as a workaround
- **Context decay** after about 20 rounds, so she starts new chats proactively
- **10 minute voice cap** cuts off long disclosures, forcing re summaries that cause detail mixups (A vs. C)
- **More detail, more errors**: long passages lead to factual mistakes
- **Voice can't match intensity**: she wants it to be strongly present when she's very angry
- **Disengages on anger** (e.g. venting about a boss); she understands why but finds it limiting

## Why she would switch to Tee

Persistent memory is the clearest switching reason: she called it "the most important" feature and already does manual work to fake it in ChatGPT.

- **Memory across sessions** without prompting, a companion that "knows what I've been through"
- **Clinician backed design** reads as reassurance, not restriction
- **Escalation to real therapists** feels like a safety net she would welcome, something ChatGPT can't offer
- **Price**: open to paying if affordable; free would be ideal
- She accepts Tee may lag ChatGPT on UI polish and speed, and volunteered to test it

**Biggest gap she named:** Tee is web only. Her habit is voice on the go, so no mobile app is a serious barrier for her.

## Tensions

The better Tee feels, the more it could delay care for people like Niki, which is the engagement vs. wellbeing question in its most concrete form.

### Therapy framing conflict

Gil wants Tee positioned as never a substitute for therapy. Niki already uses AI as one, with a human therapist only as a last resort when sleep breaks down. A label won't change her behavior; she would use Tee the same way she uses ChatGPT.

- Risk: a warmer, memory equipped Tee could push her threshold for human help even further out
- Her one therapy experience ("a mindset issue," no treatment) likely taught her therapy isn't worth the effort, so her ordering is learned, not random

### Wants vs. needs

Much of her wishlist is engagement design: a TikTok style feed from her conversations, mini games, GIFs and emojis, an always on Siri style companion. Some of these could deepen reliance rather than relieve stress. What she asks for and what helps her should be evaluated separately.

### Order effect

Niki believes people who start with AI find it natural, while people who start with a therapist find AI strange. If that holds, the human is the scary step for AI first users, and Tee may need different onboarding or escalation for each group. This is one interview's hypothesis, worth testing.

## Design opportunities for Tee

Tee's escalation path is the one thing ChatGPT can't copy, and for Talkspace a well timed handoff to therapy is both a good outcome and revenue, so healthy design and business point the same way.

1. **Lower the threshold for human help**
   - Notice patterns over time (recurring sleep issues, the same situation returning) and suggest a therapist earlier
   - Frame it as an option, not a sign of failure
   - Make first human contact feel smaller than a 1 to 2 hour session, such as a short text exchange
   - Carry context into the handoff so she never re explains to a stranger
2. **Lead with the next step.** Her main quality complaint was having to ask "what's next?" each time. A structured CCBT guide can offer a plan without being asked.
3. **Comfort first, then guidance.** The sequence she values most in ChatGPT: "you're not wrong, it's not your fault, but we can do better."
4. **Memory that holds up in long disclosures.** No caps that force restarts, no mixing up people in a story.
5. **Voice first, mobile access.** One tap launch from a widget or watch fits how she actually uses AI.
6. **Tone controls** (warmer vs. more structured) are lower risk than feeds or games and address her wish for personality.

## Research caveats

Niki is a great persona but likely not a typical user, so her findings need checking against other interviews.

- **Power user bias**: early Pro subscriber with multiple paid tools; her friends don't even notice differences between tools
- **Recruiting**: about 300 reached via Instagram story, 6 responded; a convenience sample, not representative
- **The \~1% emotional support stat** needs a cited source before it goes into the writeup; 6 of 300 matching it is a coincidence
- **Stigma likely lowered responses**: Niki hides this usage from people, and others probably do too
- **Self reported behavior**: her accounts of frequency and usefulness haven't been observed directly
