## Project description

I am redesigning Tee, Talkspace's clinically supervised AI guide for everyday stress, so that it feels as easy to reach for as ChatGPT but is built around the user's wellbeing instead of time spent in the app.

More people now open a general AI chat when something is on their mind. These tools are always there and never judge, but they rarely push back, forget context, and have no path to a human when things get worse. Tee already has what they lack: clinicians in the loop and a direct connection to Talkspace therapists. What it doesn't have yet is the pull that makes people come back to ChatGPT.

My thesis is a high fidelity mobile prototype of a reimagined Tee for adults 25 to 44 who already use AI for emotional support. It focuses on three experiences:

1. **Leading with a next step.** After comfort comes a small, concrete action grounded in CBT, so users don't have to keep asking "what should I do now?"
2. **Memory that carries the story.** Tee remembers the people and patterns in a user's life, so they never have to start over.
3. **A bridge to a human.** When patterns repeat, Tee suggests a therapist earlier, makes the first contact feel small, and hands over context so the user doesn't explain everything again.

Alongside the prototype, I will define a set of healthy engagement measures that show how Tee can succeed as a business without relying on compulsive use. Tee is not a substitute for therapy. It is a tool for the moments in between, designed to know when to step back.

## Central question

**How might an AI mental health tool be engaging enough to compete with general chatbots, while measuring its success by how well users are doing rather than how long they stay?**

Most consumer apps grow by holding attention. In mental health, that incentive can quietly work against the user. A warmer, smarter AI companion might keep someone from seeing a therapist even longer.

Tee is an unusual case where the healthy choice and the business choice can point the same way. For most AI companions, a user leaving to see a human is lost engagement. For Talkspace, a well timed handoff to therapy is both a good outcome and revenue.

My project tests this in two ways:

- **Research:** interviews with people who already use ChatGPT or Claude for emotional support, plus a comparison of general LLMs, Tee, and human therapists, to find where what users want and what Tee offers actually overlap.
- **Design:** a prototype that turns that overlap into specific features, and engagement measures that reward outcomes over time on screen.

Smaller questions I'm carrying through the work:

- What would make a paying ChatGPT or Claude user switch to Tee?
- Where is the line between a stress tool and a therapy substitute, in actual product behavior?
- When and how should Tee suggest a human so it feels like an option, not a failure?

## Motivation

I started this semester wanting to understand the emotional relationship people are forming with AI. The more I read, the more I noticed that the products people lean on for comfort are mostly designed with the same engagement playbook as social media. I wanted to work on the other side of that problem: what it looks like to design an AI people want to come back to, without making them dependent on it.

Interning at Talkspace on the Design Systems and AI team gave me a front row seat to how a mental health company thinks about AI. Then Gil, Talkspace's CTO, offered to mentor my thesis around Tee. That changed the project from a speculative concept into something grounded in a real product, real usage data, and real business constraints. Gil pushes me to think like a founder, and I want my thesis to show that design for wellbeing can also be a sound business decision.

My interviews made it personal in a different way. One participant told me AI is her main mental health support, and she only sees a therapist when stress breaks her sleep. She isn't unusual, she's just early. If tools like Tee are going to be part of how my generation copes, I want to help shape them so they lead people toward care, not away from it.

## Intended audience

**Primary: adults 25 to 44 who already use general AI chat for emotional support.** This range comes from Tee's own usage data. These are people who open ChatGPT or Claude to vent, organize their thoughts, or calm down after a hard day. Many have never seen a therapist, or only go when things feel severe. They often keep this use private because of stigma.

**Why it matters to them.** They already rely on AI for support, but the tools they use weren't designed for it. They forget context, rarely push back, and can't connect them to a human when they need one. A better designed tool could give them real help in the moment and a gentler path to care.

**Secondary: the Talkspace product team.** Through Gil, the research and prototype can inform how Tee evolves, including how it positions itself against ChatGPT and how it measures success.

**Where they'll encounter it.** On their phones, in the moments between work and sleep when something is on their mind. For the thesis itself, people will experience it as a clickable prototype and walkthrough at the ITP Thesis Week presentation, and in a case study on my portfolio.

**How I'll involve them.**

- **Interviews (ongoing):** 25 minute sessions with screened participants who use AI for emotional support. So far I've spoken with classmates Hyewon, Shloka, and Minami, and with Niki, a heavy ChatGPT user.
- **Tee trials:** participants get free access to Tee through Gil and share feedback on tone, memory, and how "AI" it feels.
- **Prototype testing:** in the spring, the same participants and new ones will test each prototype round, so the people who shaped the problem also judge the solution.
- **Weekly mentorship with Gil:** product and business feedback at every stage.

Tee's clinical infrastructure makes it one of the few products that can actually try this.

## Form

The final piece is a high fidelity mobile prototype of a redesigned Tee, presented with a short case study and a set of healthy engagement measures.

**Final outputs**

1. **Interactive mobile prototype.** Built in Figma, with key flows prototyped in code so the AI responses feel real. It covers onboarding, a full stress session, memory across sessions, and the bridge to a human therapist.
2. **Healthy engagement framework.** A one page set of measures, such as "felt better after the session" and "took the next step," that Talkspace could track instead of time in app or daily streaks.
3. **Case study and Thesis Week presentation.** The research, design decisions, and what users said, told as one story.

**Explorations so far and what I learned**

- **FigJam mind map** of the problem space. Mapping it showed me that the "companion between therapy sessions" idea overlapped too much with Talkspace's existing model, which led me to focus on stress support for people who don't have a therapist. \[Embed screenshot\]
- **Problem definition with Gil, using Tee usage data.** Users dealing with self worth in close relationships had the longest sessions and highest return rates. It also showed that the best outcomes came from longer sessions, which made me skeptical of short, swipe style engagement features. \[Embed chart or summary\]
- **Comparison of general LLMs, Ash, Tee, and therapists.** Tee's real differences are its default behavior (asking questions, noticing patterns, gentle pushback) and its infrastructure (live clinical oversight and a handoff to care). \[Embed table\]
- **Screener and 25 minute interview guide.** All five qualified respondents said they use AI to "organize my thoughts," which points to structure as a core need. \[Embed screener results\]
- **Interview with Niki.** She called persistent memory the most important feature and saw the path to a therapist as a safety net, not a turnoff. She also asked for feeds and mini games, which showed me I need to separate what users ask for from what actually helps them. \[Embed findings doc\]

## Personal Evaluation Rubric

I'll judge the thesis on whether users feel helped, whether they would choose it, whether the business case holds, and how much I grow as a product designer.

| Goal | How I'll know I reached it |
| --- | --- |
| Users feel helped, not hooked | In testing, most participants say they feel calmer or clearer after a session, and can name a next step they would actually take. |
| Users would choose it over ChatGPT | At least half of the AI users I test with say they would switch, or use Tee for emotional support instead of their current tool, and can say why. |
| The bridge to a human feels like an option | Participants describe the therapist suggestion as helpful or reassuring, not pushy or like a sign that they failed. |
| The business case holds | Gil and the Talkspace team see the healthy engagement measures as realistic to track, and agree they connect to retention or conversion to therapy. |
| The process is user led | Every major design decision traces back to something I heard in interviews or testing, and I run at least three rounds of testing in the spring. |
| I grow as a product designer | I can present the project as a founder would: the problem, the evidence, the bet, and the tradeoffs. It becomes the lead case study in my portfolio. |
