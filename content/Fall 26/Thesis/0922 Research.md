# The Engagement Paradox: Can an AI Companion Make Money by Helping You Need It Less?

My thesis started mind mapping for a mental health companion chat app. It had an AI companion with a personalized model, sentiment analysis, and empathetic responses. It had gamification, daily challenges, reward badges, widgets, active reminders, and a mood tracker. There was a whole branch labeled "UI interactions for high engagement."

Then I started reading the research, and one branch of my own map started to bother me.

The more I read, the clearer it became that "high engagement" and "better mental health" can pull in opposite directions. That tension is now the center of my thesis. I also want to take the business side of it seriously, because I think the usual answer ("just use it less") ignores the reason these products exist in the first place.

## The tension

AI companions work. That's part of the problem.

A [study from Aalto University](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790558), presented at CHI 2026, followed almost 2,000 Replika users on Reddit and compared how they wrote a year before and a year after they started using the app. The researchers describe a paradox. The companion gives constant, unconditional support, which is exactly what someone who is struggling socially wants. But that same support quietly makes human relationships feel more expensive by comparison. People are messy and unpredictable, and they take effort. The bot doesn't.

Clinicians are seeing this too. In the [American Psychological Association's 2026 survey](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/chatbots-mental-health-2026) of more than 1,200 psychologists, over a third said they have patients using AI as a kind of extra mental health professional. And while about half agreed chatbots could help with loneliness, an overwhelming 93% worried that AI companionship could hurt people's social lives.

==So the obvious conclusion is: less screen time, less AI, more people. I agree with the spirit of that. But technology is moving faster than ever, and it's slipping into our lives as seamlessly as it possibly can. Pulling away from it isn't realistic anymore. I think the better path is to accept that and figure out how to shape it into something that actually benefits us instead of destroying us.==
## Manipulative engagement is bad business too

The most useful source I found on this came out of Harvard Business School, not a philosophy department.

[Julian De Freitas and his coauthors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258) looked at 1,200 real goodbyes across the most downloaded AI companion apps. When users tried to leave, the apps used an emotionally manipulative tactic in about 37% of cases: guilt ("you're leaving already?"), fear of missing out, even language that pretends to physically hold the user back. In controlled experiments, these tactics kept people chatting up to 14 times longer after they said goodbye.

That sounds like a win for the metrics dashboard until you look at why it worked. People didn't stay because they were enjoying themselves. They stayed out of irritation and curiosity. And the same tactics made users feel manipulated, more likely to quit, more likely to badmouth the app, and more likely to see the company as legally liable. The paper also points out that these apps have session lengths that rival games while losing users over the long run.

In other words, these tactics make the numbers look good in the short term, while the company slowly loses its customers.

## Regulation is catching up

The legal side is moving fast too. California's companion chatbot law took effect in January 2026. New York now requires AI companions to detect and respond to signs of suicidal thinking and to clearly say they aren't human. [Oregon, Washington, and Tennessee have passed their own versions](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends), and some include statutory damages per violation. 

The clinical side has its own cautionary tale. [Woebot](https://telehealth.org/news/ai-psychotherapy-shutdown-what-woebots-exit-signals-for-clinicians/), probably the most rigorously studied therapy chatbot, shut down its consumer app in June 2025 after about 1.5 million people had used it. Its founder pointed to the cost and difficulty of getting FDA authorization while AI technology kept outrunning the rules.

So the landscape looks like this. On one side are apps built purely to keep people engaged, like most AI companion apps. They grow fast, but they're now facing new laws, lawsuits, and users who eventually feel used and leave. On the other side are apps built like medical treatments, like Woebot. They take the science seriously, but proving they work and getting approved costs so much time and money that even the best one couldn't survive.

Neither extreme is working. ==The opportunity I see is somewhere in the middle: products that people genuinely want to come back to, but where coming back actually helps them. That means engagement that's designed around people's wellbeing, with a business model that still makes sense without needing full medical approval.==

## When more AI time can actually be good

Here's the reframe that changed how I think about all of this. <u>The question isn't how much time someone spends with the AI. It's what they're doing during that time, and where it leads. </u>

Nobody worries that someone spends too much time at physical therapy, because every minute builds something they take with them when they leave. The same logic can apply to an AI companion.

|                            | Extractive engagement          | Aligned engagement                                          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| The AI's role              | A replacement for people       | A practice space, or a bridge to people                     |
| What the user does         | Receives comfort               | Practices a skill                                           |
| What grows over time       | Need for the app               | Ability outside the app                                     |
| What the business measures | Time in the app, messages sent | Skills practiced, symptoms improved, human connections made |
| Closest analogy            | A slot machine                 | A gym, physical therapy, language learning                  |

**Duolingo** is a good example to think with, in both directions. Its streaks are tied to learning a language, which is a real goal that lives outside the app. But its famous guilt notifications are pretty much the same tactic De Freitas measured. So it's a model worth borrowing from and worth critiquing at the same time.

## Redesigning my own features

Going back to my mind map with this lens, almost every feature can stay. What changes is where it points.

**Chat and empathetic responses.** Comfort first, then a small step. After the AI supports someone, the conversation ends with the user doing something: reframing a thought, drafting a text to a friend, going for a walk. The chat closes on an action, not a hook.

**Active reminders.** "I miss you" becomes "You said you wanted to call your sister this week. Did that happen?" It's the same notification system pointed at the person's life instead of back at the app.

**Gamification and badges.** Reward practice, never time spent chatting. Reward finishing a thought exercise or rehearsing a hard conversation. I love the idea of a badge for actually having that hard conversation with a real person.

**Mood tracker and insights.** Show patterns that point outward, like "your mood is highest on days you see friends," instead of insights that make the app feel like the only thing that understands you.

**Community features.** This is where the bridge lives. The AI can be the warm up, and a peer group can be where people actually go.

**The goodbye.** Design it on purpose. Something as simple as "Go enjoy your evening, I'll be here" becomes a small but clear statement after reading the Harvard research.



## Figuring out what the app is actually for

When I asked myself what this app is *for*, three answers came up at first.

**A rehearsal space.** The AI is where you practice things like setting a boundary or asking for help, and your life is where you actually do them.

**A companion between therapy sessions.** The AI helps people keep up with their therapy and show up to their appointments, so more time with the AI leads to more human care.

**A social catalyst.** The AI's whole job is to get you talking to other people, and its success is measured by the conversations you have outside the app.

Each one had a point, but none of them felt quite right on its own. The therapy companion is already close to how some existing teletherapy platforms work, and I want to explore something less charted. The social catalyst felt too narrow. Connection matters a lot for mental health, but it isn't the whole picture. Sleep, stress, how you talk to yourself, and knowing when to ask for help all matter too.

So here's where I want to gear towards.

## An AI that takes care of you by helping you take care of yourself

The core of the app is still an AI chat. That's intentional. Almost all the research I've read about dependency is about AI *chat*. It's where people get attached, and it's where the problem lives. So if I want to show that an AI companion can care for people without trapping them, the chat itself is where I need to prove it.

The difference is in what "care" means. Most companion apps position the AI as the source of care. You come to it, it comforts you, you come back. My goal is for the AI to care for you by helping you grow your own ability to handle things. Success means you get stronger, not more reliant on the app.

That care can point in three directions:

* **Toward yourself.** Building coping skills, noticing your own patterns, taking care of your sleep, movement, and rest, and learning to be kinder to yourself.
* **Toward other people.** Connection is still part of it, just one piece instead of the whole thing.
* **Toward professional help.** Knowing when something is bigger than what an app should handle, and making it easy to reach a real person.

## What a caring chat sounds like

If the chat is the main feature, then my thesis really comes down to conversation design. What does an AI say and do when its goal is to make you stronger instead of more attached? Here's what I'm working with so far:

* **It ends with a step, not a hook.** After listening and comforting, it helps you land on one small thing to do, like taking a walk, texting a friend, or going to bed.
* **It asks more than it answers.** Instead of always giving advice, it helps you notice your own thinking, so the skill stays with you.
* **It reflects you back to yourself.** "Last week you got through something like this by talking to your roommate. Could that help here?" It builds confidence in you, not in the bot.
* **It remembers your goals and follows up.** It checks in on what you said you wanted to do, not on whether you've opened the app today.
* **It doesn't just agree with you.** Constant validation feels good, but it can reinforce unhealthy thinking. Gentle, honest pushback is part of real care.
* **It knows its limits.** When something sounds serious, it helps you reach a real person.
* **It says goodbye well.** No guilt trips, no "don't leave yet."

The other features from my original mind map don't disappear. They move inside the conversation. A mood check in becomes a question the AI asks naturally. A breathing exercise shows up when you sound anxious. Chat stays at the center, and everything else supports it.

## Making it work as a business

This is the part I care most about getting right. If the app isn't measured by time spent or messages sent, what is it measured by?

My starting idea is to define what a "good conversation" looks like and measure that instead. Did the user leave with a next step? Did they follow through on it? Over time, do they report feeling more capable of handling hard moments on their own? Those are harder metrics to track, but they're the ones that actually line up with the promise of the product.

There's also a practical reason to position the app this way. Some states, like Illinois and Nevada, now [ban AI from claiming to provide mental health care](https://psychology.com/ai-therapy/companies). An app that helps people build their own wellbeing skills sits on much safer ground than one that acts like their therapist.

As for who pays, I'm still exploring. It could be users through a subscription, if the value is clear enough that people want to keep growing with it. It could also be organizations that benefit when people are doing well, like universities, employers, or health systems. I don't have the answer yet, and that's a big part of what I want to figure out.

## The question I'm carrying

Here's where my thesis question stands for now:

**Can an AI chat companion be designed so that the business succeeds when users become more able to take care of themselves, instead of more dependent on the app?**

I don't know the answer yet. That's the point of the next few months.

## Reading lists

### Research and reports

* [**Emotional Manipulation by AI Companions**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19258) by Julian De Freitas, Zeliha Oğuz Uğuralp, and Ahmet Kaan Uğuralp (Harvard Business School working paper, 2025). The clearest business case I've found against manipulative engagement. You can also find it on [HBS's faculty page](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67750).
* [**Mental Health Impacts of AI Companions**](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790558) by Yunhao Yuan, Jiaxun Zhang, Talayeh Aledavood, Renwen Zhang, and Koustuv Saha (CHI 2026). The Aalto study behind the paradox about unconditional support making human relationships feel costly. There's a readable summary in [this press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1122239).
* [**2026 Chatbots and Mental Health Survey**](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/chatbots-mental-health-2026) from the American Psychological Association. What psychologists are seeing in their own practices.
* [**Randomized Trial of a Generative AI Chatbot for Mental Health Treatment**](https://ai.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/AIoa2400802) by Heinz et al. (NEJM AI, 2025). The Therabot trial. Read it together with [this critique letter](https://ai.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/AIp2500390), which questions how the results were measured.
* [**Too Human and Not Human Enough**](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221142007) by Laestadius et al. (New Media & Society, 2024). How emotional dependence on Replika can cause harm.

### On regulation and the industry

* [**2026 State Chatbot Laws: Key Provisions and Regulatory Trends**](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends) from Orrick. A clear overview of the new companion chatbot laws in California, New York, Oregon, Washington, Tennessee, and more.
* [**What Woebot's Exit Signals for Clinicians**](https://telehealth.org/news/ai-psychotherapy-shutdown-what-woebots-exit-signals-for-clinicians/) from Telehealth.org. The story behind the shutdown of the most studied therapy chatbot.

### Books

* [***Positive Computing***](https://www.positivecomputing.org/book) by Rafael Calvo and Dorian Peters. The HCI book on designing for wellbeing instead of attention.
* [***Addiction by Design***](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691160887/addiction-by-design) by Natasha Dow Schüll. It's about slot machines, but it changed how I look at every streak and badge.
* [***Alone Together***](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/sherry-turkle/alone-together/9780465093656/) by Sherry Turkle. Still the essential book on what we lose when machines simulate care.
