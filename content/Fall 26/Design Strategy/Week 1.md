# Michael Porter's 1996 HBR classic, "What Is Strategy?"
(https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy)

Porter's core argument is that **operational effectiveness** (doing the same things better than rivals, faster, cheaper, higher quality) is not strategy. It's necessary, but everyone can copy it eventually, so it just raises the bar for the whole industry without giving anyone a lasting edge. He calls this trap "**competitive convergence**," where companies racing to benchmark and imitate each other end up looking more and more alike.

**Real strategy**, in his view, means choosing to be different. That means either performing **different** activities than competitors, or performing similar activities in **different** ways. He grounds this in examples like **Southwest Airlines** and **Ikea**, showing how their advantage comes from a whole tailored system of activities, not any single feature.

A few of the key ideas :

Positioning can come from three sources: 1)the variety of products offered, 2)the needs of a customer group, or 3)how customers are accessed (geography, scale, etc).

Trade-offs are what make a position **defensible**. If you can do everything at once with no sacrifice, competitors can just copy you. Real strategy means deliberately choosing what not to do.

Fit matters more than any single strength. A strategy's activities should **reinforce** and **complement** each other, so imitators have to match the **entire system**, not just one piece, which is much harder.

He ends by warning that the **biggest threat to strategy** usually isn't competitors, it's internal pressure to grow, chase every customer, or blur the position over time.


**Variety based positioning** is about the product or service itself requiring a unique system of activities to deliver well. That's different from **needs based positioning** (serving all the needs of one customer group) and **access based positioning** (serving customers who are reached differently, like rural versus urban).
