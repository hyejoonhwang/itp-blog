## Intro
- The future shows up first as small, weird experiments at the edges (the **fringe**). When you connect enough of them, you see a **trend**.
- Being successful can make you stop looking. BlackBerry compared _products_ instead of watching the _trend_.
- A futurist doesn't predict the future like a psychic. She gathers evidence, spots trends, and figures out which futures are more or less likely.
## Chapter 1: The drone that reshaped America (in theory)

Webb says you need to be **"chronologically ambidextrous."** That means handling today's work with one hand while thinking about the far future with the other, like a pianist playing different parts with each hand.

- Every decision touches other systems, so zoom out and follow the ripple effects.
- Warning signs that look separate are often one pattern. Sony saw a string of isolated attacks instead of a trend.
- Our brains resist change and get stuck in the present. You need a deliberate process to get around that.
- **Webb's six step method** (the rest of the book):
    1. Find the fringe
    2. Look for patterns (CIPHER)
    3. Ask hard questions to test the trend
    4. Figure out timing
    5. Write future scenarios and a strategy
    6. Pressure test the strategy


## Chapter 2: Why we still don't have flying cars

### The flying car story

Since 1910, inventors have promised flying cars about once a decade. There have been prototypes, magazine features, and even one made from a Ford Pinto attached to a Cessna plane. Investor Peter Thiel famously complained that we wanted flying cars and got Twitter instead. Flying cars have become shorthand for "the future let us down."

### The moving sidewalk story

Now go back to Chicago in the 1890s. The city was packed with people, the streets were unpaved and covered in horse manure, and walking was miserable. A New Jersey inventor named Alfred Speer had an idea: what if the sidewalk moved _for_ you?

He built a moving sidewalk for the 1893 World's Fair. It ran along a 3,500 foot pier and had a faster express lane with benches. People loved it. Paris built a fancier one in 1900, and there were plans for Manhattan, the Brooklyn Bridge, Boston, Detroit, and more. By 1905, everyone thought moving sidewalks were the future of getting around.

Then they faded. They were noisy and broke down, nobody had a plan for Chicago snow, and shop owners hated them. Meanwhile, a few engineers at the fringe were tinkering with gasoline engines. Most people couldn't imagine riding in a heavy metal box at 40 miles an hour, and that's what won.

Neither moving sidewalks nor flying cars were ever the trend. They were _versions_ of a trend. The real trend is **autonomous transportation: getting around while needing to pay less and less attention to it.** We keep imagining that future through pictures we already know (a car, but in the sky!) instead of following the boring real progress: sensors, software, and mapping.


### Technology never changes alone

Webb lists ten forces that shape every trend: **wealth distribution, education, government, politics, public health, demography (who the population is), economy, environment, journalism, and media.**

She goes back to Sony. In the years before the big hack:

- Hackers were gathering on new anonymous sites like 4chan and Reddit, and there were anti Sony forums on Reddit as far back as 2009.
- Americans deeply distrusted the government after the Iraq War and Hurricane Katrina.
- The mortgage crisis wiped out jobs and savings, and Occupy Wall Street was aimed at banks and corporations.

Angry people with free time, new places to organize, and a big corporate target: Sony could have seen it coming.

More examples of these forces in action:

- Japan has so many elderly people and so few young workers that robots will have to fill the gap. So if you want to know the future of robots, look to Japan, not just Silicon Valley.
- Terrorist groups became social media experts. Webb calls them "hackerrorists."
### Why good trends are hard to spot

Everyone can picture the Jetsons' flying car. Nobody gets excited about "a new stability sensor linked to a car software update." Real trends are often **boring**. For example:

- Only 24% of 16 year olds got driver's licenses in 2014, down from 46% in 1983. That's a quiet signal that young people may care less about owning cars.
- In _The Graduate_ (1967), a character's one word of career advice is "plastics." Plastics went on to make personal computers possible and also created an enormous floating garbage patch in the Pacific. Nobody planned for either.
- Mobile phones took about 60 years to go from car phones to everyday devices, with lots of dull improvements along the way.
### What to take away

- Don't confuse the flashy _version_ of something (trendy) with the underlying _direction_ (the trend). Ask what human need is really driving it.
- A trend is a lasting change, not a hot product.
- Always look at the forces outside technology too.
- Timing matters, so place trends into time zones.

## Chapter 3: The card company that lasted 125 years vs. the computer giant that vanished

### Nintendo's story

Nintendo started in 1889 in Kyoto making paper playing cards with flower designs. In the 1950s, the founder's grandson noticed new signals: plastics were becoming common, TVs were getting cheaper, kids loved cartoons, and Disneyland was about to open. So Nintendo:

1. Made plastic cards.
2. Signed a deal with Disney to print characters on them, which opened the card market to kids.
3. When card sales flattened, turned an engineer's side project, a stretchy grabbing arm toy, into a million seller.
4. Moved into early video game machines.
5. Created Donkey Kong, Mario, and Zelda, then the Game Boy, and later the Wii.

At each stage, it kept watching what was changing around it and moved early.

### DEC's story

In 1937, a Bell Labs mathematician built a calculating machine at his kitchen table using phone parts and a tobacco tin. World War II poured money into computers, and they slowly grew from room sized calculators into programmable machines.

Two MIT engineers founded DEC to make smaller "minicomputers" for labs and businesses. It became hugely successful, with 120,000 employees and $14 billion in sales. But in 1977 its president said, more or less, that nobody needs a computer at home. Meanwhile, two guys named Steve were building a computer in a garage, portable computers were appearing, and early online message boards had started. DEC was sold off in 1998.

### Why smart people get it wrong

Experts judge the future through their own expertise:

- In 1995 one internet pioneer predicted the internet would collapse the next year, because he was focused on its outage problems.
- A famous tech writer called the idea of online shopping and online communities "baloney."
- On the other side, techno optimists promised the internet would free everyone from government. Also wrong, just in the opposite direction.

### Probable, plausible, possible, and why you need all three

DEC did great by focusing only on the _probable_ future: slightly better computers for businesses. That works for a while. But when a _plausible_ future showed up (regular people wanting computers at home), it couldn't make the jump. Allowing some room for risky futures is what kept Nintendo alive.

### The moon story

Could a computer have predicted the moon landing? In 1961, President Kennedy had just been humiliated by a failed invasion of Cuba, and the Soviets had just sent the first human into space. He stood before Congress and asked for a man on the moon before the decade was out.

At that point:

- No rocket was powerful enough to get there.
- No spacesuits existed that could keep astronauts safe outside the ship.
- No communication system could reach back to Earth.

An algorithm could have calculated odds on the engineering. It couldn't calculate Kennedy's charisma, the country's excitement, or its appetite for risk. For Kennedy, the moon was a **preferred future**: a goal you set first and then work backward to reach.

### What to take away

- Watching trends is a survival skill for any organization.
- Experts are often _more_ blind because of their deep expertise.
- **Probable futures** help in the short term. **Plausible and possible futures** keep you alive in the long term.
- Algorithms can help, but they can't read human factors. There's also the **"genie problem"**: a machine gives you exactly what you ask for, even if you asked for the wrong thing.

## Chapter 4: Finding the fringe (Step 1)

### The monkey story

Two monkeys, Mango and Romeo, sit in separate rooms. Neither can move its arms. Each controls one direction of a digital arm on a screen, one up and down and the other left and right, using only its thoughts, through tiny implants in its brain. To earn a sip of orange juice, they have to coordinate with each other, brain to brain, over the internet.

A Duke scientist named Miguel Nicolelis spent 25 years on this. He started in the late 1990s, when the hottest gadget was a phone with a built in fax. People thought his work sounded like a horror movie. But he was trying to help paralyzed people walk. In 2014, a young man paralyzed from the waist down, wearing a mind controlled robotic suit Nicolelis's team built, kicked the first ball of the World Cup. Later the team networked rat brains together into a "Brainet" that solved problems better than any single rat.

This is **the fringe**: scientists, artists, and oddball thinkers testing ideas that sound absurd today. It's where every trend is born.


### Why normal research misses it

Focus groups and market research look _backward_. By the time regular people can talk about something, it's already mainstream. You also have to define your topic broadly. If you only watch your own industry, you'll miss the ideas coming from the edges.


### The unusual suspects

Some of history's biggest breakthroughs came from people others dismissed:

- **Galileo** was put under house arrest for saying Earth goes around the sun.
- **Jane Goodall** had no degree and lived among chimps, giving them names instead of numbers. Academics laughed at her until she proved chimps eat meat and make tools.
- **Isaac Asimov** was on the subway with no story idea for a meeting with his editor. He flipped open a random book, saw a picture of a fairy queen kneeling to a soldier, and let his mind wander from armies to feudalism to the fall of Rome to outer space. By the time he arrived, he had the outline for his famous _Foundation_ series. His method was to ask **what if** and **why**, not _how_.

### The Dolly story

A quiet British scientist, Ian Wilmut, spent years trying to engineer sheep that could produce human medicines. In 1996 his team cloned an adult sheep, Dolly. The world panicked:

- Ethicists said the reaction should be horror.
- The Church of Scotland asked the UN for a ban on human cloning.
- President Clinton banned federal funding for human cloning.
- Polls showed most Americans suddenly opposed cloning and genetically modified food.

Scientists had talked about cloning back in the 1970s and then dismissed it as science fiction, so nobody was ready. Today, most packaged food in the US contains genetically modified ingredients, and hardly anyone thinks about Dolly.

Then it happened again. In 2015, Chinese researchers used a new gene editing tool called **CRISPR** on human embryos. The scientific world was shocked again, because experts had been focused on their own narrow research and weren't watching the fringe.


### The biohacker story

Zoltan Istvan believes technology can help humans live indefinitely. He drove to a DIY lab in California and had a small chip injected into his hand, so he can unlock doors and phones with a wave. Sounds crazy? Webb imagines where it could go: a chip that tracks your heart and automatically calls 911 with your location during a heart attack. Then she lists questions for 17 different fields (doctors, insurers, lawyers, retailers, schools, police) to show how this "weird" thing could affect all of them.

### The tool: a fringe sketch

Put your topic in the center. Around it, draw the people and organizations involved (nodes) and the relationships between them (connectors). Things happening now go close to the center, and things further off go in outer rings.

Three rules while sketching:

1. Include theoretical or even shaky information.
2. Assume today's obstacles might be solved later.
3. Assume that if something can be hacked or repurposed, it will be.

Five questions to fill it in:

1. Who is working on this, directly or indirectly?
2. Who is funding or encouraging it?
3. Who will be directly affected?
4. Who has reasons to fight it, because they'd gain or lose something?
5. Who might see it as a starting point for something bigger?

Then, go back and mark which items are things you actually **know** versus things you're **assuming**.

### Things that started out weird

Anesthesia, plastic surgery, and antidepressants all began as fringe experiments:

- Early surgery was so painful that one major hospital did roughly one operation a month. Doctors experimenting with ether were mocked at first. Today, anesthesiologists are among the best paid doctors in America.
- Plastic surgery went from fixing duelists' noses, to secret Hollywood procedures, to reality TV.

The **seven stages of acceptance** go from "Never heard of it, why should I care?" all the way to "How did I ever live without it? Why didn't I think of that?"


### What to take away

- Trends are born at the fringe, so go looking there on purpose.
- Keep an open mind: today's taboo is often tomorrow's normal.
- Map the whole ecosystem, not just the technology.
- Fringe researchers usually ask whether something _can_ be done, not whether it _should_. That second question is your job.

## Chapter 5: Solving the Google mystery (Step 2)

### The opening scene

In 2015, a California police officer pulls over a small, gumball shaped car for driving too _slowly_. There's no driver. It's a Google self driving car. No ticket was issued, and Google joked about it online.

Webb says the car isn't the interesting part. The real question is: **why is a search engine company building a car?**

### Collecting clues

She lays out everything Google was doing:

- It bought eight robotics companies, including the maker of those creepy four legged robot videos.
- It invested in Uber and a gene editing startup.
- It worked on floating internet balloons (Loon) and even a space elevator.
- It launched Google Glass.
- It launched Gmail in 2004. The announcement came out on April Fool's Day, so people thought it was a joke. It offered huge free storage, and in return it scanned your emails.
- It launched a free phone information service, GOOG-411. At a 2007 conference, a Google executive admitted the real point was to collect millions of voice samples to train speech recognition.

Sitting at a later conference, Webb sketched all this out. The picture that appeared: Google was building an **"exo brain,"** a digital assistant that knows your calendar, emails, voice, and location, and helps before you even ask. In 2012 Google launched Google Now, which did exactly that. The trend was **ubiquitous virtual assistants**.

### How pattern spotting works

Our brains are wired to find patterns. Webb describes "computer glasses that cover your whole field of view." You probably pictured Google Glass, but she meant a VR headset. Your brain fills in gaps from past experience, sometimes wrongly.

The Kanizsa triangle illusion makes the point: three Pac Man like circles make you "see" a triangle that isn't drawn. You have to look at the individual pieces _and_ the shape they make together. **Zoom in, then zoom out.**


### CIPHER, her pattern checklist (with the Google examples)

- **Contradictions:** things that shouldn't go together. Big companies handed their emails and documents to a search engine.
- **Inflections:** a moment that speeds everything up. Around 2005, researchers embraced neural networks, and computers got smarter fast.
- **Practices:** something that challenges "the way things are done." Talking to your computer instead of typing.
- **Hacks:** people building workarounds because existing tools frustrate them. Frustrated developers built early assistant apps like Siri.
- **Extremes:** pushing into new territory. Google trying to know you better than you know yourself.
- **Rarities:** odd outliers that turn out to matter. A free phone service didn't make business sense, unless it was secretly about collecting voices.

Not every trend shows all six.


### But why was Google doing all this?

Larry Page wanted to tackle huge, world changing problems, and that takes enormous money. Google's money comes from ads. The more people online and using Google, the more ad money, and the more moonshots it can afford.

That explains cheap Android phones, fast fiber internet, and internet balloons for remote areas. And self driving cars mean you can spend your commute on YouTube and Gmail instead of the road.

Page's **"toothbrush test"**: would you use it once or twice a day, and does it make your life better? The goal is to become invisible infrastructure, like plumbing, which you never think about but can't live without.

### What to take away

- A single event (the police stop) is a distraction. Collect lots of clues, then zoom out.
- Use CIPHER to find the pattern in the noise.
- Always ask _why_ a player is doing something, because that reveals where the trend is heading.

## Chapter 6: "Uber for X," or how to not get fooled (Step 3)

### The opening scene

Around 2010 to 2017, everyone in business kept saying "we're the Uber for ___." There were more than 500 startups on one investor site alone: Uber for dog walking, laundry, haircuts, flowers, private jets, and ice cream. Someone even wrote a comic poem about it.

A news executive tells Webb, excitedly, that his company will be "the Uber for news." She asks what that means. It turns out he just means an app, "but more like Uber."

### Uber's actual story

- Founder Travis Kalanick had two earlier file sharing startups. One got sued into bankruptcy, and the other sold for $19 million.
- In 2010 he started a ride service, and top investors like Sequoia passed on it. They loved the app but couldn't picture it being more than another black car service.
- By 2015 Uber was valued at $62.5 billion, the most valuable private startup in the world.
### Rewinding to 2010 to see why Uber worked (CIPHER applied)

- **Inflection:** The recession pushed unemployment to 10%. Lots of people needed extra income, and apps like TaskRabbit and Airbnb appeared.
- **Contradiction:** Even in a bad economy, iPhone sales jumped 91%.
- **Contradiction again:** New York City issued 11,787 taxi medallions (the permits needed to run a cab) in 1937. Decades later there were only about 13,000, even with millions more people and tourists. Each permit cost around $800,000, which is why getting a cab in the rain was hopeless.
- **Practices:** Uber went around the whole taxi system of permits and dispatchers.
- **Hacks:** Payment was automatic. You just got out of the car, and the receipt arrived by email.

Uber is so convenient that at a 2016 tech conference, one attendee complained online that Uber "robbed" him with surge pricing, while posting a receipt showing he paid $47 for a ride that normally cost $9. People keep using it even when it hurts them.

### The trap

When a phrase is everywhere, our brains assume it must be a real trend. That's the **availability heuristic**: judging something as likely just because examples come to mind easily. Add **belief bias**, accepting ideas that fit what we already believe, and everyone gets on the bandwagon together.

### The cautionary tale: "flash sale for X"

Just before the Uber craze, the hot phrase was flash sale sites: designer goods at big discounts for 24 to 48 hours. Here's how that played out:

- Gilt was valued at $1 billion, and One Kings Lane at over $900 million.
- Zulily's stock jumped 71% on its first trading day, making its founder a billionaire.
- Copycats sprang up for wine, surf gear, fishing gear, and more.

Webb deliberately stepped back and asked what would have to be true for this to last. The answer: not much. The flash sale model was a response to a temporary bad economy, and it was easy to copy. Regular stores started running their own discounts. Gilt later sold for $250 million, Zulily's shares crashed, and One Kings Lane went looking for a buyer.

### Her method: argue against yourself

Webb did competitive debate for eight years. In debate, you take the other side's claim and try to poke holes in every part of it. She calls these "disads" (disadvantages). You start with one big question: **what would have to be true for this to be a real, lasting trend?** Then you break it into smaller questions:

- Is it habit forming? Does it pass the toothbrush test? Would people keep using an "Uber for alcohol" if it charged surge prices every Saturday night?
- What real human need does it meet, or does it just serve laziness in a busy moment?
- Will it last once the economy improves?
- Did it grow out of real technology that keeps evolving, or is it just copycats?

### The verdict

"Uber for X" fails too many of these questions. It's **trendy, not a trend**, and most of those copycat startups will fail.

But Uber _the company_ is different. It's quietly building the real trend: **automation, and transportation as a service**. In 2015 it hired away 40 top robotics researchers from Carnegie Mellon to work on self driving cars, and put up recruiting billboards near the campus.

Webb imagines a plausible 2040. Self driving Uber vehicles take you to work and pick up your kids. Groceries and medicine arrive by drone. On a potato farm, self driving tractors plant, drones watch the crops, machines harvest and wash the potatoes, and self driving trucks take them to a chip factory and then to stores. The whole farm needs maybe one skilled human. In this future, Uber is the invisible delivery layer under daily life, the way Google is the invisible information layer.

### What to take away

- Just because everyone's talking about something doesn't make it a trend. Catchy phrases make you lazy.
- Before you believe a trend, try hard to **disprove it**. Ask what would have to be true, and test each piece.
- The hot copycat idea is usually a distraction. The real trend is often hiding behind the company everyone is copying.

## The thread running through all of it

Every story has the same shape. Someone either **zooms out and connects dots early** (Webb in Tokyo, Nintendo, Google) or **stays focused on what's in front of them and gets blindsided** (BlackBerry, Sony, DEC, the scientists shocked by Dolly and CRISPR). The six steps are a structured way to make sure you're the first kind.
